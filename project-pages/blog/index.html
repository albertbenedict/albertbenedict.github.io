The Lazy Perfectionist
Have you ever been in that situation where you have a specific goal, you have everything planned out perfectly, but then the next day comes and for some reason you end up doing none of it? I've been in that situation countless times, whether I'm trying to learn a new language, practice my drawing skills, or starting a new project. Apparently, there is a term for this: the "lazy perfectionist."
Now, what exactly is a "lazy perfectionist"? In short, it's someone who desperately wants to get things done, and in a very particular way, but can't seem to actually do them. They have such high expectations that they become worried they won't be able to reach that standard. As a result, they end up procrastinating and abandoning the things they planned.
Here is what happened to me. I started taking a computer science course, and after the first week, I was hooked. I thought, "I can't wait to finish this course and build this and that." With that ambition, I was determined to crush my goal. But after week two, I started running into problems that completely halted my progress. My immediate thought was, "Damn, it really isn't that easy. Maybe it's just not for me." So, I took a "sabbatical" break which ended up lasting for months.
Two Kinds of Perfectionism
Given all that, it's easy to assume perfectionism is a bad thing that just leads to procrastination and laziness. But is perfectionism really that bad? Well, according to research, it depends. Perfectionism isn't a single, uniform trait, it actually consists of two distinct dimensions:
Perfectionistic strivings (setting high personal standards and aiming for excellence). This is the healthy, highly productive side of perfectionism. It is driven by intrinsic motivation where you want to do a good job because you genuinely care about the quality of the work and the process of mastering a skill. People who demand high quality from themselves are actually less likely to procrastinate. This isn't just a feeling, a 2018 meta-analysis found perfectionistic strivings were linked to less procrastination, while perfectionistic concerns were linked to more of it. When you operate from strivings, you are process-oriented; you set the bar high, but your self-worth is not tied to being perfect during the process.
Perfectionistic concerns (excessive worry about making mistakes and harsh self-criticism). This is the paralyzing, toxic side of perfectionism. It is driven by fear and ego, specifically the fear of making mistakes, letting others down, or discovering that you aren't "good enough." When you operate from concerns, you are entirely outcome-oriented, believing that if the result isn't perfect, it is completely worthless.
How I Sabotaged My Own Progress
Honestly, it didn't take long to figure out which side of the fence I was on. If you want to see how fast these 'concerns' can completely derail a project, here's another time I got in my own way. About two years ago, I did a 30-day challenge where I forced myself to draw every single day for a whole month. Before starting, I was firmly in that "perfectionistic strivings" mindset. I had high standards for myself, but I was focused entirely on enjoying the process and putting in the reps. And honestly? It went unbelievably well. I drew every single day, made huge progress, and by the end of the month, I could actually draw pretty well. I showed my progress to my friends and relatives, and they were blown away. They praised it and acknowledged how far I'd come. So, it's a success story, right?
Well, no. That's exactly where it all started to go wrong. Receiving all that praise somehow completely flipped my mindset. I had high expectations but was still focused on the process, but suddenly, my mindset shifted. I set the bar even higher, but this time, it came with an extra weight: the terrifying possibility of not reaching that new goal. Worrying about falling short made every mistake feel massive. The slightest setback hurt deeply and made me second-guess myself: "Am I really good enough?" "Can I actually do this?" "Maybe I just can't do it." That single shift in mindset from process to outcome completely cost me my momentum and my progress. I ended up taking a month-long break, and that daily drawing habit completely fell apart. While I still try to sketch every now and then, I lost that effortless drive I had during the first 30 days. But looking back, it helped me realize something crucial. The problem wasn't that my standards were too high, the problem wasn't that I was a "perfectionist." The problem was that my ego got involved, and I started measuring my self-worth by the final result instead of the effort.
Getting Out of the Trap
So, how do we fix this? How do we escape the trap of the "lazy perfectionist" and start using those healthy strivings as fuel? The answer isn't to lower your ambitions or to just "try harder." It actually comes down to two main things:
Keep the standards, but drop the criticism. Some research suggests that to beat this, you don't have to give up your high standards, you just have to change how you react when you fall short. Instead of harsh self-punishment, it often comes down to practicing self-compassion. If you remove the fear of being punished for a mistake, your brain stops treating the work like a threat, and the urge to procrastinate fades. In other words, you just have to give yourself permission to make a messy, imperfect attempt without immediately convincing yourself that you're a failure.
Ignore the audience. Research backs this up too, one 2024 study found that procrastination was linked specifically to socially prescribed perfectionism, the belief that others expect you to be perfect, tied to how much external praise and expectation you're carrying. To get past the paralysis, you have to stop working for the praise of others and reconnect with your intrinsic motivation. You have to do it for you. When your goal is simply your own quiet progress rather than a round of applause, the heavy pressure disappears, and the actual work becomes enjoyable again.
Where I'm At Now
And looking back at my own "journey", the research is spot on. Here is how I've actually started putting it into practice. Currently, I'm focusing heavily on my software engineering skills, specifically web development. I still have those high standards, and I still want to build all the cool things I've always wanted to make. But my approach to getting there has completely changed. Instead of obsessing over the finish line, I'm taking it day by day, step by step. I've accepted that I will make mistakes along the way, and more importantly, that making mistakes isn't actually a bad thing. In fact, noticing your own shortcomings is the mandatory first step to improving. There is this quote by the legendary Formula 1 driver Niki Lauda that sums this up perfectly: "Winning is one thing, but out of losing I always learned more." While in this case, it isn't exactly about "winning" or "losing," the point still stands when it comes to succeeding and failing. You just have to train yourself to view these setbacks and mistakes not as proof that you aren't good enough, but as the exact tools you need to get better. Above all, the biggest change has just been learning to be kinder to myself. Yes, you should always strive to do well, but I've realized something incredibly freeing: sometimes, doing your best is actually a lot better than achieving "the best."
